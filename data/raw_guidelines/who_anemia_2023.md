# WHO Guidelines: Nutritional Management of Iron Deficiency Anemia (2023)

## WHO_IDA_P01: Diagnostic Thresholds and Staging of Iron Deficiency
The World Health Organization establishes that:
- Serum ferritin < 15 ug/L (or ng/mL) in non-pregnant adults reflects depleted iron stores. In clinical practice, values < 30 ug/L in the presence of inflammatory markers indicate functional iron deficiency.
- Hemoglobin (Hb) levels below 12.0 g/dL in non-pregnant females and below 13.0 g/dL in males indicate anemia. Severe anemia is defined as Hb < 8.0 g/dL, requiring prompt clinical intervention.

## WHO_IDA_P02: Enhancers of Non-Heme Iron Bioavailability
Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) is the most potent dietary enhancer of non-heme iron absorption. Co-ingestion of at least 50 to 75 mg of ascorbic acid (e.g., from bell peppers, citrus fruits, tomatoes, or dark leafy greens) converts ferric (Fe3+) iron to the more absorbable ferrous (Fe2+) state, overcoming the inhibitory effects of dietary phytates and polyphenols.

## WHO_IDA_P03: Inhibitors and Meal Timing Separation
Dietary factors that severely inhibit non-heme iron absorption include:
- Polyphenols and tannins found in black tea, green tea, and coffee: These bind non-heme iron in the intestinal lumen into insoluble complexes, reducing absorption by 50% to 70%.
- Calcium from dairy products (milk, cheese, yogurt) or calcium supplements: Calcium competitively inhibits both heme and non-heme iron transport via divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1).
- Clinical Recommendation: High-tannin beverages and dairy products must be separated from iron-rich meals or iron supplements by at least 1.5 to 2 hours.

## WHO_IDA_P04: Recommended Dietary Iron Targets
For individuals diagnosed with iron deficiency or mild anemia, dietary iron intake should meet or exceed 18 mg/day for adult females of childbearing age and 10 mg/day for adult males, complemented by medical iron supplementation when indicated by the supervising dietitian or physician.
